Nobody replaces their office chair at the right moment: they either keep it for years too long, with a sagging seat and their back paying for it, or they throw it out at the first rattle without knowing the fault is cheap to fix. Shops settle the question with a vague "5 to 10 years" that tells you nothing, because a chair does not die all at once: it wears out part by part, and each part warns you before it fails for good.

I use a Herman Miller Aeron every day, a chair with a 12-year warranty. That figure is my yardstick: a manufacturer's warranty is the most honest clue you have about how long it expects the chair to last. For the other models I mention I have no direct use: I analyse them by their official specs and the patterns repeated in verified reviews, and I say so when it matters.

## The short answer: it is not years, it is the warranty

There is no single number of years that fits everyone. An office chair's lifespan depends on three things: your daily hours, your weight and the build quality. A remote worker who sits for 8 hours a day wears a chair out far sooner than someone who uses it two hours a day, and a 90 kg person punishes the gas lift and the foam harder than a 60 kg one.

If you need a quick reference, look at the **warranty** before the price:

- **3-year warranty** (for example, the [SIHOO Doro C300](/en/catalog/chairs/sihoo-doro-c300/)): the maker trusts the product, but is not betting on a decade.
- **5 years** (the [Hbada E3 Pro](/en/catalog/chairs/hbada-e3/)): a reasonable middle ground for the mid range.
- **10 years** (the [FlexiSpot C7](/en/catalog/chairs/flexispot-c7/) or the [IKEA MARKUS](/en/catalog/chairs/ikea-markus/), which costs little more than €150): a frame built to last.
- **12 years** (the [Herman Miller Aeron](/en/catalog/chairs/herman-miller-aeron/), the [Haworth Zody](/en/catalog/chairs/haworth-zody/) or the [Steelcase Leap V2](/en/catalog/chairs/steelcase-leap-v2/)): the premium standard, with chairs that in practice pass a decade with ease.

The warranty is not a promise that nothing will break: it is the manufacturer's confidence in its own frame. And that already separates chairs built to last from disposable ones.

Mind one nuance: long warranties usually cover the frame and the mechanisms, not the foam or the upholstery. That is why a premium chair can have a sagging seat at five years even with a twelve-year warranty: nobody answers for that part. Seat wear is almost always what sets your real replacement date, and it sits apart from the warranty.

## Why "5 to 10 years" is misleading

The range shops repeat mixes €100 chairs with €1,000 chairs, which is why it tells you nothing about *yours*. Three reasons that number falls short:

- **The frame is not what fails first.** A chair can "hold up" for ten years because the skeleton and base are still intact, while the seat foam already collapsed in year three. The chair is still there, but it no longer supports you.
- **Daily use changes everything.** Manufacturers calculate lifespan with offices in mind, not intensive remote work. Eight hours a day, five days a week, wear more than occasional use.
- **Weight dictates the gas lift and the foam.** It is the factor that shortens seat life the most, and it is almost never mentioned.

If your chair is uncomfortable but not broken, the problem is probably not that it expired, but that it is badly set up. Before writing it off, check [how to adjust an office chair](/en/guides/how-to-adjust-office-chair/).

## What wears out first, part by part

The order in which a chair fails is fairly predictable, and knowing it helps you decide:

| Component | Wear sign | Repairable? |
|---|---|---|
| **Seat (foam)** | It sags, you feel the base when sitting | Rarely worth it on cheap chairs |
| **Gas lift** | The chair sinks on its own | Yes, a standard and cheap part |
| **Casters** | They jam, squeak or stop rolling | Yes, swapped in minutes |
| **Armrests** | They loosen or peel | Sometimes, by brand |
| **Lumbar / mechanism** | It loses tension or stops reclining | Depends on model and parts |
| **Upholstery / mesh** | Frays, unravels or loses firmness | Hard to fix properly |
| **Base / frame** | Creaks, flexes or has play | No: this is the end |

Note one detail: the two parts people replace most (the gas lift and the casters) are also the cheapest to swap. Do not throw out a chair just because it "sinks": that is a worn gas lift, and it is replaced for little money.

## Repair or replace: the golden rule

Not every fault justifies a new chair. The decision comes down to two questions:

- **Is the part swappable?** Gas lift, casters and armrests are usually standard parts sold separately. If that is where the fault is, repair.
- **Does the frame give way?** If the base creaks, the backrest flexes or the skeleton has play, there is no repair worth doing: that is the sign the chair reached the end.

A third nuance matters: the sunken foam of an €80 chair is not repaired, because a new seat costs close to the whole chair. On a premium chair with a 12-year warranty, the same fault is covered or worth fixing. The chair's value decides whether repair is good business.

Take a concrete case: a mid-range chair with a sunken seat and an intact frame. Replacing the whole seat costs almost as much as a new chair of the same band, so it does not pay off. If instead the chair sinks on its own and the rest holds up, a new gas lift brings it back to life for a fraction of that cost. The difference between throwing it out and fixing it is not in the fault, but in which part fails.

## Maintenance that extends its life

Most chairs die from neglect before real wear. Four gestures that add years:

1. **Clean the mesh and the seat** now and then. Dust and grit act like sandpaper on the fabric.
2. **Tighten the screws** once a year. Small plays turn into big creaks if you let them.
3. **Do not drop yourself into the seat.** The impact concentrates force on the gas lift and the base.
4. **Shift position** and avoid leaning on the same side every time. Uneven foam wear comes from that.

If you also want the wear not to turn into pain, the [desk stretches and chair exercises](/en/guides/desk-stretches-office-chair/) routine helps you avoid loading the same spot all day.

## How long it should last for what you pay

As an honest orientation, no magic numbers:

- **Entry range (up to ~€150):** the [IKEA MARKUS](/en/catalog/chairs/ikea-markus/) gives a 10-year warranty and is the exception that proves the rule. Most chairs at this price last 3-5 years before the foam gives in.
- **Mid range (~€300-500):** chairs like the [FlexiSpot C7](/en/catalog/chairs/flexispot-c7/) (10-year warranty) or the [Hbada E3 Pro](/en/catalog/chairs/hbada-e3/) (5 years) are built for 5-10 years of daily use. It is the sweet spot for remote work.
- **Premium (€800 and up):** the [Steelcase Leap V2](/en/catalog/chairs/steelcase-leap-v2/) documents durability of 15-20 years, backed by the 12-year warranties of the Aeron, Zody and Steelcase. You pay to never think about your chair again for over a decade.

## Checklist: repair or replace?

Before buying anything, run this check on your current chair:

1. Sit and press the seat: do you feel the base, or is it still firm?
2. Raise the chair and wait a few minutes: does it sink on its own?
3. Roll the casters over the floor: do they roll or drag?
4. Grab the armrests and push: do they move too much?
5. Hold the backrest and wiggle it: does it flex or stay firm?

If only one of points 2 to 4 fails, you have a cheap repair ahead. If point 1 or 5 fails, or several pile up, it is time to renew.
