Most home office pain does not come from one dramatic mistake. It comes from small mismatches repeated for hours: a laptop screen that is too low, a chair that leaves your feet hanging, a desk that forces your shoulders up, a mouse too far away, and breaks that never happen.

This guide gives you a practical order for setting up an ergonomic home office. Start with the adjustments that cost nothing. Buy equipment only when the setup shows you a real limitation.

I am not a physiotherapist. I am a software engineer who has worked from home since 2019, made the usual mistakes, and rebuilt my setup after neck, lower-back and wrist discomfort. If you have sharp pain, numbness, pain travelling down an arm or leg, or symptoms that do not improve after reasonable adjustments, speak to a qualified health professional.

## The five-minute posture check

Sit down exactly as you normally work. Do not correct yourself first. Then check these five points:

1. **Feet:** both feet flat on the floor or on a footrest.
2. **Knees:** roughly at a right angle, with no hard pressure under the thighs.
3. **Lower back:** supported by the backrest, not floating away from it.
4. **Elbows:** close to the body, with forearms roughly parallel to the floor.
5. **Screen:** top third of the display around eye level, without bending your neck down.

If two or more of these fail, your setup is probably creating extra strain before the workday even starts.

## The correct order: chair, desk, screen, keyboard

Ergonomics works better when you adjust the setup in order. If you start with the monitor, then change chair height later, the screen will be wrong again.

### 1. Set chair height first

Raise or lower the chair until your feet can stay flat and your thighs feel supported without pressure behind the knees. If the desk is too high and you need to raise the chair, use a footrest so your feet do not hang.

Your lower back should touch the backrest. If your chair has lumbar adjustment, move it until it supports the natural curve of your lower back rather than pushing into the wrong spot.

### 2. Match the desk to your elbows

When your shoulders are relaxed, the desk surface should sit close to elbow height. If the desk is too high, you will lift your shoulders. If it is too low, you will round forward.

With a fixed-height desk, you usually adjust the chair to match the desk, then solve foot position with a footrest. With a height-adjustable desk, set the desk around your seated elbow height first, then save that position.

### 3. Raise the screen

Laptop-only work is the most common home office posture trap. The keyboard and screen are attached, so one of them will always be wrong: either the keyboard is comfortable and the screen is too low, or the screen is high and the keyboard is unusable.

The simplest fix is a laptop stand plus an external keyboard and mouse. Your screen should be about an arm’s length away, with the top third of the display close to eye level.

### 4. Bring keyboard and mouse closer

Your elbows should stay near your torso while typing. If the mouse is too far away, the shoulder works all day without you noticing. Keep keyboard and mouse at the same height, close enough that your wrists stay neutral.

Avoid raising the rear feet of a keyboard if it bends your wrists upward. A flatter or slightly negative typing angle is usually easier on the wrists.

## A seven-day reset before buying new gear

Before ordering a new chair or desk, test your current setup for one week.

| Day | Adjustment | What to watch |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Take a side photo of your real posture. | Forward head, raised shoulders, back away from the chair. |
| 2 | Fix chair height and foot support. | Do your feet stay flat and relaxed? |
| 3 | Raise laptop or monitor to eye level. | Does neck tension change by the afternoon? |
| 4 | Move keyboard and mouse closer. | Do shoulders and wrists feel less loaded? |
| 5 | Use a 50-minute work / 10-minute movement rhythm. | Does lower-back discomfort arrive later or feel milder? |
| 6 | Check lighting and screen glare. | Are you leaning forward or squinting? |
| 7 | Repeat the side photo. | What improved, and what still cannot be fixed with your current equipment? |

At the end of the week, you should know whether the real bottleneck is the chair, the desk height, the screen, accessories or movement habits.

## The movement rule that matters

The best posture is not one perfect position. It is the next position.

Even a good setup becomes uncomfortable if you hold it for hours. Use a simple rhythm: after 45 to 60 minutes of focused work, stand up, walk, stretch or change position for a few minutes. You do not need a complicated routine; you need consistency.

If you have a standing desk, use it in short blocks rather than forcing yourself to stand all morning. If you do not have one, walking around the room, refilling water or doing a short stretch still helps.

## Common mistakes in home offices

### Working from the sofa “just for a bit”

The sofa encourages a rounded back, low screen angle and unsupported wrists. Fine for a short email, bad for real work. If space is limited, a small folding desk and a basic chair usually beat the sofa.

### Treating the chair as the whole solution

A better chair helps, but it cannot fix a laptop screen that is 25 cm too low or a desk that forces your shoulders up. Chair, desk, monitor and habits work as a system.

### Ignoring small warning signs

Tingling fingers, persistent neck tightness, morning stiffness or headaches from the base of the skull are not productivity badges. Adjust the setup early, and get professional help if symptoms persist.

## When buying equipment does make sense

Buy only after you know the limitation:

- **Laptop stand:** if the screen is too low.
- **External keyboard and mouse:** if laptop-only work bends your neck or wrists.
- **Footrest:** if your feet do not reach the floor after chair height is correct.
- **Monitor arm:** if the screen stand cannot reach the right height.
- **Ergonomic chair:** if your current chair lacks stable lumbar support or cannot match your body size.
- **Standing desk:** if you need easier position changes during the day.

## Final checklist

Use this as a quick monthly audit:

- Feet fully supported.
- Lower back touching the backrest.
- Shoulders relaxed.
- Elbows close to the body.
- Wrists neutral.
- Screen raised to eye level.
- Keyboard and mouse close.
- Movement break every 45 to 60 minutes.

You do not need a perfect home office to work comfortably. You need a setup that fits your body, enough movement to avoid staying locked in one position, and the discipline to fix small problems before they become chronic ones.
