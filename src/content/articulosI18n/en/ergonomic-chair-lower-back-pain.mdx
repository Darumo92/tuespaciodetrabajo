Lower back pain from sitting is almost never a sign that you need a more expensive chair: it is almost always a mismatch between the chair's lumbar support and the height of your own curve. The lumbar is not at the same height on every body. If the support sits too low, it presses your sacrum and holds nothing; if it sits too high, it pushes your vertebrae and you end up separated from the backrest. In both cases your lower-back muscles do the work of holding you up for the 7-8 hours of the workday, and that is the ache you feel by the end of it.

I use a Herman Miller Aeron with PostureFit SL, and even then the support has to be placed at the height of my curve, not at the factory default. For the other models I mention I have no direct use: I analyse them by their official specs and the patterns repeated in verified reviews, and I say so when it matters. This is a guide to understanding your pain and deciding about your chair, not a medical diagnosis: if the pain radiates, wakes you at night, or does not improve in 2-3 weeks, see a doctor before a furniture shop.

## Why your lower back hurts when you sit

The lumbar spine has a natural inward curve (lordosis). When you sit, especially if you lean forward or slide down the seat, that curve tends to flatten and the pressure on the intervertebral discs rises compared to standing. [Spine-health](https://www.spine-health.com/wellness/ergonomics/office-chair-how-reduce-back-pain) puts it plainly: sustained static posture is what punishes the lower back the most.

Two nuances people forget:

- **The chair does not "hold" you by itself.** Lumbar support only works if it is in the right place. A mis-set support is worse than none, because it makes you adopt an awkward posture without noticing.
- **Mechanical pain warns you late.** You do not feel the damage in the first hour, but accumulated at the end of the day. That is why people buy any "ergonomic" chair and do not understand why it still hurts.

If your pain is more general (neck, mid-back, stress or lack of movement on top of the lumbar), I have a full guide on [back pain from working at home](/en/guides/back-pain-working-from-home/) that covers all the causes. Here I get specific: the lower back and the chair.

## The adjustment that matters most: lumbar support in height (and depth)

It is the first thing to check and the one that most separates a real chair from one that only carries the "ergonomic" label. There are three levels:

- **Fixed lumbar.** The support sits at a single height. Fine if it happens to match your curve; otherwise it is decoration. Most gaming chairs and budget models fall here.
- **Height-adjustable lumbar.** You can raise or lower it until it meets your curve. This is the minimum I would ask for on long days.
- **Dynamic lumbar.** It adapts on its own as you shift position, without touching levers. It is what the PostureFit SL on my Aeron does, and what far cheaper alternatives now offer.

The test to know if it is well placed: sit with your back against the backrest. You should feel the support right on the curve (the spot that sinks in just above your belt), not on the sacrum and not on the vertebrae. If you have to push your stomach forward to "make a posture", it is wrong.

A note on the **depth** of the lumbar: as important as height is that it does not push you too hard. Some lumbar pads protrude so much they force you to overarch your back. Look for one that is also depth-adjustable, or a dynamic one that spreads the pressure instead of digging it in.

## The other three adjustments that hurt your lower back

The lumbar support does not work alone. These three can be sabotaging it without you noticing:

### Seat depth

If the seat is too deep, the edge presses the back of your knees and, to compensate, you slide forward and lose the lumbar support. If it is too short, your thigh is not supported and your lower back takes the whole load. Ideally you want **2-3 fingers** between the edge of the seat and the back of your knee, with your back supported. Adjustable depth solves it in two seconds; it is one of the real gaps on my Aeron.

### Seat height

Here is a mechanism almost nobody explains: if the seat is too low, your knees rise above your hips and your pelvis tilts back. Result: the lumbar curve flattens and the lumbar support, however well placed, stops touching where it should. Set the height so your feet are flat on the floor and your knees at 90 degrees. The exact chair-to-desk relationship is covered in our [correct desk and chair height guide](/en/guides/correct-desk-and-chair-height/).

### Recline

Reclining the backrest a few degrees (not lying back, just opening it slightly) shifts part of your torso's weight off the lumbar discs and lets the muscles rest. That is why a chair with a tension-adjustable recline is worth it for lower back pain: it lets you change posture through the day instead of staying locked in the same position for 8 hours. If your chair does not recline, or tilts as one block, that is the next thing to improve.

## When the chair is not the problem

An honest warning before you spend money: if your chair already has well-placed adjustable lumbar and it still hurts, the problem is probably not the chair.

**Sitting for long stretches is aggressive on the lower back, period.** The best chair on the market does not replace getting up. The practical rule is no more than 50 minutes straight without standing; when you stand, the pressure on the discs redistributes. If you want to switch postures without breaking your work, a standing desk helps, but it is not essential: a timer and a two-minute walk already change the pattern.

**A lumbar cushion is a patch, not a solution.** On a dining chair it can be an acceptable stopgap for a few weeks, but on an already ergonomic chair it only adds another surface that slips out of place. If you depend on the cushion, your chair is not holding you.

If the pain comes from staying still too long, look first at the [desk stretches and chair exercises](/en/guides/desk-stretches-office-chair/) routine: for mechanical lower back pain it is the cheapest intervention there is.

## Which lumbar to choose for your case

It is not better or worse in the abstract, but which type of support fits what hurts:

- **Pain at the end of the day, a back that "sags":** try a dynamic lumbar that adapts on its own. The [SIHOO Doro C300](/en/catalog/chairs/sihoo-doro-c300/) is the reference mid-range option, and at the premium end there is the [Haworth Zody](/en/catalog/chairs/haworth-zody/) with its PAL lumbar that adjusts each side separately.
- **You want to fix the support at one height and forget it:** the [FlexiSpot C7](/en/catalog/chairs/flexispot-c7/) lets you lock the lumbar or leave it free, with a ten-year warranty.
- **You want to fine-tune everything without paying premium prices:** the [Hbada E3](/en/catalog/chairs/hbada-e3/) brings a three-zone adaptive lumbar with six height levels.
- **Your sacrum hurts as well as your lumbar:** the sacrolumbar support of the PostureFit SL on the [Herman Miller Aeron](/en/catalog/chairs/herman-miller-aeron/) is unlike anything else, at the cost of its price and no seat-depth adjustment.

## A 5-minute checklist for your lower back

Before buying anything, run this on the chair you already have:

1. Sit with your back against the backrest and find the lumbar support: do you feel it on the curve, or too low or high?
2. Check for 2-3 fingers between the seat and the back of your knee, with your back supported.
3. Feet flat and knees at 90 degrees: if your knees rise, raise the seat.
4. Recline the backrest a few degrees and check you keep the lumbar support as you lean back.
5. If after adjusting everything your lower back still hurts, schedule a break every 50 minutes before you buy.

If your chair will not let you do point 1 or 2, there is your answer to why it hurts: it is not your back, it is an adjustment that does not exist. And once you have the chair, the order for setting it up properly is in [how to adjust an office chair](/en/guides/how-to-adjust-office-chair/).
